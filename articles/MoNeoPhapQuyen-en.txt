The Anchor in the Philosophy of Right
Nguyễn Phước Lộc
Loc Nguyen’s Academic Network, Vietnam

The foundational proposition in Hegel's philosophy of right (Hegel & Bùi, 1970, p. 100):
"What is rational is actual and what is actual is rational."

It follows:
"The identity of actuality, which is existence, and rationality."

So how is rationality interpreted? Hegel stated:
"Rationality is the identity of the identical and the non-identical."

What are the identical and the non-identical? Hegel stated:
"Rationality is the identity of the universal moment and the particular moment."

This means:
"The essence of actuality, which is existence, is the identity of the 'in-itself, self-defining, subjective, infinite' and the 'for-itself, differentiating from others, objective, infinitely finite'."

This proposition summarizes and is equivalent to the Hegelian dialectical model:
"Spirit (Geist) is the essence of existence, a process of self-development and self-consciousness, passing through four distinct but inseparable stages: 1) consciousness, 2) self-consciousness, 3) reason, 4) absolute spirit. Each stage is manifested through phenomena, and these phenomena develop according to the dialectical model: a later stage of cognition negates the preceding stage to reach a higher level while still containing elements of the former stage (determinate negation). At the same time, the later stage, although a result of the former, reverts to become the basis for the earlier stage (the law of reciprocal action)."

In other words, rationality has begun to connect with Hegel's phenomenology of spirit and the Hegelian dialectical model:
"The identity of the universal moment and the particular moment is a summary of the dialectical model, so rationality is the inevitable result of the dialectical model."

Now we understand existence, existence as natural essence, rationality, and the dialectical model, but do the thoughts and ideas that humans conceptualize exist as a natural essence? Hegel stated:
"The Idea is the absolute unity of the concept and existence; in other words, the Idea is the true concept."

This proposition is important because it connects the concept and existence so that concepts in human thought also have a natural essence like all natural phenomena. At this point:
"The Idea is existence, it is existence as natural essence, it is also rationality, it is also the phenomenon of spirit, it is the substance of spirit, so it is also spirit."

It follows:
"Hegel has mapped the first natural world, which is the material world, into human thought to become the spiritual world, and this spiritual world is regarded as a second natural world with a natural essence like the first natural world."

It follows:
"The Idea belongs to the second natural world, and Hegel's philosophy of right is deployed in this second natural world (the spiritual world) without losing its inherent, constant, natural essence."

So how is right defined? Hegel stated:
"Right is any immediate existence of free will, so right is freedom as the Idea."

In short, right is an existence that has free will, but how is it related to the Idea? We need to understand a subtle inference from rationality:
"Because rationality is the identity of the universal moment and the particular moment, the Idea must have some power to form and realize this identity, and that power is will."

But at this point, we cannot yet link right to the Idea in the spiritual world, so we need an anchor to anchor right to the Idea, and that anchor is the justification of existence:
"The Idea always has the power of will. Willpower always tends toward freedom, so will and freedom are two sides of the same coin. In other words, will is the subject of freedom (Hegel & Bùi, 1970, p. 131) and freedom is the aspiration of will, and we can extend that will is also the justification for existence (justification of existence)."

Accordingly:
"The justification of existence is the justification for existence, the rationale for existence, the reason for existence, the struggle for existence, the purpose of existence of an entity, the aspiration for the existence of an entity. Moreover, although the justification of existence is regarded as will, it can also be regarded as the motive/driver of will, which is something immediately preceding will but is not different from it. In other words, the justification of existence is the substance of will."

It follows:
"The Idea and will merge into one, with will being the justification of existence for the Idea. The will of human beings is called free will."

In other words:
"The Idea is no different from will, no different from free will, no different from the justification of existence."

At this point, right belongs to the spiritual world:
"Right is the Idea, and is called the Idea of right because of the hidden nature of the justification of existence. We can also understand right as a label placed on the Idea at the precise point of free will, or more accurately, right is a perspective on the Idea at the point of 'justification of existence' to express/deploy a great rationale, to perform a great struggle, a great justification in a great court of objective spirit/absolute spirit (phenomenology of spirit). If that justification is not rational, then that Idea/right is destroyed—that is the punishment."

From this, I assert that:
"Hegel's philosophy of right has the miraculous quality of spanning from abstract right to morality & ethical life to positive right, and this spectrum of right simultaneously has a reciprocal nature, meaning that positive right is thought back to abstract right to reflectively approach the finite objective spirit with the infinite subjective spirit as the supreme judge of right. Each segment of the spectrum of right, such as abstract right, morality & ethical life, and positive right, are also Ideas that have two qualities, universal and particular, and the rationality of the Idea is the identity of these two qualities, noting that the process of achieving this identity through the dialectical model, if locally spanned, also has a reciprocal nature."

Note:
"It is because of the connection between the rationality of the Idea and Hegel's phenomenology of spirit that definitions of world spirit, objective spirit, subjective spirit, and absolute spirit appear in Hegel's philosophy of right."

The process of spanning right into abstract right, morality, and ethical life is interpreted as follows:
"Assuming the Idea is the final, total result, its justification of existence or free will is labeled as right, as the core of the Idea, at which point the label of right is called abstract right, representing the immediate existence located at the highest level—the first level within the Idea's locality (because there is still absolute spirit/objective spirit as the whole Idea). Abstract right, as an Idea, is always rational, and is therefore also called rational right. Hegel continues to divide the Idea just as one divides the content of a book (Hegel & Bùi, 1970, pp. 182-184) according to an inverted triangle model with the foundation at the apex. When the Idea continues to reflect to identify its own essence and differentiate itself from others, the inner part of the Idea contemplates this essence through an intermediate realm with the right of others to establish a connection, yet the Idea remains in its own inner part (temporarily called the good part) which means it still places more emphasis on the in-itself existence rather than the for-itself existence. In other words, at this point the Idea establishes morality, which is located at the second level of the self-development process of the Idea of right (abstract right), and expresses the basis of the connected order of abstract right. So morality is the essence of the Idea derived from abstract right, expressing the particular existence of the Idea (Hegel & Bùi, 1970, p. 182). The Idea—free will continues to contemplate the good part (the inner part) which has already been reflected upon and is now reflected onto others (the world), which means the Idea, while still not having left itself, has stepped into the world, moving toward a dialectical moment to fully synthesize the two universal and particular moments, at which point the Idea is omnipresent both in-itself and for-itself, making free will both a substance and a necessity. In other words, morality is concretized into ethical life, which is located at the third level of the self-development process (Hegel & Bùi, 1970, p. 183)."

After that:
"Ethical life, actualizing itself to become an ethical force, is then concretized in detail from the family to civil society to the state."

Hegel's philosophy of right is the Idea of right in social history, it is also the Idea of social history that actualizes itself through the cunning of reason:
"The true Idea, that is, right, actualizes itself through the so-called cunning of reason, where the Idea has the power to use others through their motives to actualize itself."

It follows that Hegel cannot deny the material world, so he mapped the material world (the first natural world) into the spiritual world (the second natural world) while preserving its inherent, constant, natural essence. Therefore, Hegel's philosophy of right, although coherent in the spiritual world, only applies to social history, even though this is still correct and sufficient. However, it is interesting and useful to extend right into the material world (the first natural world) where there is no more perfect way than to unify matter and consciousness. Accordingly, I propose the following theory of the Idea:
"The world is an Idea, humans are an Idea, a tree is an Idea, an axiom is an Idea, everything is an Idea. Each Idea 'is,' 'lives in,' or 'has an illusion in' its own copy of the world, where each copy is both unreal and real; they are bubbles. Only we as the Idea, through will (free will), justify our own existence, which transforms into the justification of existence for countless other Ideas. But the other Idea-bubbles cannot be known (do not exist) to us. This is the core of the theory of the Idea, in which: consciousness and matter are one, or more moderately, the boundary between matter and consciousness is blurring. The philosophy of right studies the true Idea of right as a reference for positive right and studies the Idea of right with free will as a reference for the justification of existence."

It follows:
"With the theory of the Idea, there is only one specific person A as one Idea A, one Idea-bubble A that contains everything, and this human Idea-bubble A is the universe-bubble A, where everything in the universe-bubble A changes continuously, randomly with will through the continuous, random interference with will between Idea A and all other Ideas. In other words, person A 'creates' ('perceives') everything in bubble A through Idea interference, and everything is an Idea, which in Hegel's terms is created by the cunning of reason through person A. But in reality, there is no true cunning of reason in this theory of the Idea because Idea A 'creates' ('perceives') everything, and bubble A contains everything."

It follows:
"With the theory of the Idea, there is only one unique right A with the Idea-bubble A, which then spans into an abstract right A, a morality A, an ethical life A, and is then concretized into an ethical force A, and is then further concretized in detail into a family A to a civil society A to a state A, culminating in an absolute spirit A."

Accordingly, we also have:
"If bubble A is destroyed, everything, from abstract right A to the state A to absolute spirit A, is destroyed, dissolving like a bubble. But this destruction does not exist for A because A is the universe-bubble/Idea, and the existence of bubble A is self-consciously known by A because human A, although unable to know other Idea-bubbles, is self-conscious of his own existence because A is within A—we are within ourselves. Idea-bubble A only exists for Idea A."

Furthermore, we deduce that:
"The justification of existence is the anchor of the philosophy of right, and at the same time becomes the foundation of the theory of the Idea, thereby expanding the philosophy of right from the spiritual world to the material world for a simple reason: the theory of the Idea is unifying matter and consciousness; there is only one unique world, that is the world of the Idea with only one specific human being. Moreover, a specific human being A is expanded into a specific living organism A or more generally—a specific entity A, which is the general meaning of the theory of the Idea."

References
